## 2. Thesis argument in one paragraph

The thesis argues that the conventional BEV powertrain — discrete inverter, on-board charger, DC/DC converter and balancer connected to a monolithic series/parallel battery pack — should be replaced by a distributed architecture built from repeated, identical, low-voltage modules. The load-bearing definition appears in §1.4 (p. 34): "the powertrain is composed of modular elements referred to as unified power units (UPUs). Each UPU consists of a single battery cell or a small group of cells integrated with its own power electronics. These UPUs are interconnected to form a unified powertrain that combines the functionalities of multiple components used in conventional architecture." With appropriate control the same hardware drives the motor, charges from the grid, supplies auxiliary loads and self-balances, eliminating the dedicated DC/DC converter and external balancer (pp. 34–35). The claimed system benefits are scalability to high voltage using cheap low-voltage semiconductors, fault bypass and graceful degradation, high-quality multilevel output waveforms without bulky filters, high partial-load efficiency across the drive cycle (versus six-pack IGBT inverters that fall from ~97 % at rated power to ~80 % at 10 % load, p. 33), maintainability, and recyclability of small identical units (pp. 34–36). The remaining chapters then systematically remove the obstacles to this architecture: discrete UPU design practice (Ch. 2), monolithic integration of the power submodule including on-chip galvanic signal isolation across floating domains (Ch. 3), sensor-free modulation for equal energy extraction (Ch. 4), a high-speed switched-capacitor active balancer (Ch. 5), and near-perfectly coupled low-impedance magnetics for magnetic balancers (Ch. 6).

## 3. Chapter-by-chapter walk

### Chapter 1 — Overview and Objectives (pp. 25–38)

**Claim.** BEV adoption is limited by charging time, range and price premium (Deloitte 2025 survey, p. 30); BEV manufacturing cost is ~11 % above ICEV (ICCT), the gap sitting entirely in the powertrain ($11,030 vs $6,800), of which the battery pack is ~72 % and power electronics ~12 % (pp. 30–31). Power electronics, though a small cost fraction, leverage every other subsystem (efficiency shrinks the pack; balanced SoC extends range and life; uniform extraction eases cooling; consolidation removes cabling, pp. 31–32). Conventional-architecture shortfalls (pp. 32–34): six-pack IGBT inverters reach ~97 % at rated power but ~80 % at 10 % load, 80–95 % over WLTP; the trend to 800–900 V packs (Table 1.1, p. 34: Taycan, Lucid Air, EV6, IONIQ 6, BYD HAN, XPENG G9) demands costly high-blocking-voltage devices; poor recyclability; permanently live high-voltage DC after a crash. §1.4 (pp. 34–37) gives the UPU answer and thesis roadmap (Fig. 1.13, p. 38).

### Chapter 2 — A Discrete Cell Scale System: Challenges and Considerations (pp. 39–62)

**Claim.** Distributed battery inverters are an emerging industrial trend, but no certified product serves BEVs; a discrete UPU is therefore designed end-to-end to expose the real engineering challenges.

**State of the art (pp. 39–42).** Module level: Element Energy (claims up to 50 % lifetime and 50 % energy-throughput gains), Stellantis/Saft/CNRS IBIS (since 2022), TAE ACi Pack, Scalvy (claims 5× footprint and 30 % capex reduction, AC to 3 kV). Cell level: SwitchESS (15 % lifetime claim), BAVERTIS/Pulstrain, Hagal, Exro Cell Driver. Only three certified products: Instagrid (20 × 2 kWh modules, 3.6 kW discharge / 1 kW charge), SAX Power Home (5.76 kWh, 4.6 kW), Relectrify AC1 (~4,000 individually controlled cells; up to 20 % more usable energy, 40 % less degradation over 20 years). "Significantly, none of the companies with certified products currently serve the BEV sector, underscoring a critical gap in the market" (p. 41).

**Topology assessment (pp. 42–47).** Multilevel architectures are justified for low-voltage BEV use via [12]: a 7-level Si CHB beats six-pack IGBT and SiC inverters on drive-cycle efficiency (Fig. 2.4, p. 43); its inverter cost is 41 % above IGBT but 32.6 % below SiC, and with the battery included it yields the lowest total cost (Figs. 2.5–2.6, p. 44). Of six compared topologies (Fig. 2.7, p. 45), series-connected-cell types (flying capacitor, diode-clamped, P2) are rejected as still centralised; isolated-cell types (MMC, BM3, CHB) are built from identical battery-integrated submodules (states in Table 2.2, p. 46: MMC half-bridge bypass/series-positive; BM3 adds parallel, one extra switch; CHB H-bridge adds series-negative, two extra switches). In three-phase use CHB needs half the submodules of MMC/BM3 for the same line-to-line voltage because it generates negative levels natively (p. 47). The CHB H-bridge is selected for the discrete UPU.

**Case-study UPU design (pp. 47–62), key decisions.**
- *System configurations* (Fig. 2.9, p. 48): one-cell-per-UPU (highest fault tolerance, highest cost), parallel-group-per-UPU, and series-string-per-UPU (high voltage, low current, but circulating-current risk); combinable (Fig. 2.10, p. 49).
- *UPU anatomy* (Fig. 2.11, p. 50): battery, power semiconductors, gate drivers, isolation stage, signal conditioning, protections, local microcontroller, supply regulators, terminals, thermal management.
- *Supply architecture* (Fig. 2.12, p. 51): externally distributed isolated supplies vs autonomous UPUs powered from their own cell; the autonomous option is adopted — each UPU then needs a **digital isolator** to recover the central controller's signal across the isolation boundary (pp. 51–52). This is the requirement Chapter 3's OCI later internalises on-chip.
- *Battery* (pp. 52–54): pouch cells chosen; four 43 Ah same-side-tab pouch cells in parallel per UPU, giving **≈620 Wh capacity and 620 W / 1.24 kW continuous/peak (1C/2C)** (p. 54).
- *Microcontroller* (pp. 54–55): STM32G4A1KEU6 (170 MHz, 11 ADC channels, I2C/SPI/CAN/USART).
- *Supplies* (pp. 55–56): battery range 2.5–4.2 V; ISL9120IRTNZ fixed 3.3 V regulator (≤800 mA); LTC3122EDE 10 V gate-drive rail (>400/600 mA at 2.5/4.2 V input). The 10 V rail guarantees PMOS turn-off margin (V_GS,off ≈ 5.8 V at full charge, threshold −1.5 V).
- *Power switches* (pp. 56–57): low-side NMOS SiSA40DN (20 V, ~1.1 mΩ at V_GS = 10 V, 162 A continuous); high-side PMOS DMP2003UPS (−20 V, 4 mΩ at V_GS = −2.5 V, 2.5 mΩ at −4.5 V). The NMOS-low/PMOS-high half-bridge removes any need for bootstrap or isolated high-side supplies (p. 59).
- *Signalling* (pp. 57–59): dedicated point-to-point lines scale badly; I2C over two shared lines adopted; the open-drain Si8606AD-B-IS digital isolator (two I2C channels at 1.7 MHz, two 10 Mbps channels) selected because push-pull isolators short the bus.
- *Gate drive* (p. 59): SN74HC125PWR buffers into two 2EDN7524GXTMA1 dual 5 A drivers.
- *PCB and assembly* (pp. 59–62): battery tabs pass through PCB slots; copper clamp pieces double as busbars and thermal spreaders; nine-step assembly (Fig. 2.18, p. 61) ends with an enclosure that acts as the heat sink.

### Chapter 3 — Integrated Power Sub-module Design: A Unified Perspective (pp. 63–126)

This is the chapter the per-slot proposal leans on most heavily: monolithic integration of the UPU power electronics in **130 nm BCD**, including the on-chip transformer digital isolation (OCI).

**Motivation (pp. 63–67).** From the cost model of [16] (100 kW/600 V automotive inverter): at high production volumes power components dominate (>2/3 of cost), so cheap low-voltage devices plus multilevel architectures attack cost directly; integration also collapses signalling and housing costs (pp. 63–64). A structured PMIC literature review (Table 3.1, p. 67) covers 13 works spanning 3-level NMOS bucks (85.5 % at 20 MHz), a 0.18 µm 80 V BCD dual-active-bridge PMIC (50 W, 1 MHz, 94 % peak, air-core transformer + digital isolator), MISO CPV bucks (>92–95 %), and packaging/thermal studies — establishing that watt-to-tens-of-watt integrated stages with isolation are feasible but that nothing addresses cell-scale multilevel BEV submodules.

**Technology (pp. 68–69).** 130 nm Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS; only the low-voltage 5 V CMOS subset is used. The NMOS has an N-type buried layer (NBL) for isolation. Parasitic-diode management rules: substrate to the lowest system potential, NBL to the highest local potential, bodies tied to sources (pp. 68–69).

**(c) The integration-boundary principle (pp. 69–71).** The chapter's governing design rule, directly reusable in the proposal. Two constraints bound the integration level: (i) total stacked voltage on one die must stay below the process breakdown (each submodule sits at cell voltage, 4.2 V); (ii) current-path analysis distinguishes *orthogonal* currents (die↔package, unavoidable) from *lateral* currents, which may flow either through external conductors or through IC metal. "Since IC metal layers are relatively thin, their current-handling capability is limited. For applications requiring high current, it is preferable to use external conduction paths, such as reinforced PCB traces, to minimize resistive losses" (p. 70). Pulling the other way: "it is advantageous to integrate components that can share circuitry onto the same die" — the two half-bridges of one CHB level share control logic, communication and monitoring, whereas two full-bridges share little. Hence: "the optimal level of integration should be determined by carefully balancing voltage limitations, current-handling capability, power loss, and the opportunity to eliminate repeated circuitry within the system design" (pp. 70–71). In short: integrate enough to delete repeated control/support circuits; never so much that IC metal becomes the power bus.

**Feasibility per topology (pp. 71–73).** Half-bridge/full-bridge: fully feasible; the isolated secondary's substrate pins are tied to the central controller ground. MMC: feasible; the bottommost ground is a guaranteed minimum-potential node for the shared substrate. CHB: no terminal has a fixed reference, so the shared substrate must float — flagged as needing experimental verification, and a SUB pin is brought out for arbitrary biasing (p. 73, p. 77).

**Circuits designed (pp. 73–89).** Five circuit sets: three half-bridges (one per modulation scheme), a four-level MMC, and a five-level CHB. Building blocks with measured-in-simulation characteristics: inverter cell (t_PHL = 54 ps, t_PLH = 83 ps), NAND (100/107 ps), XOR (90/350 ps), D flip-flop (Figs. 3.12–3.15, pp. 79–80); NAND-based non-overlap dead-time generator using a 1 kΩ/1 pF RC giving **≈90 ns dead time** (p. 80); a level shifter (after [75]) translating global-ground commands to each MMC level's local ground (pp. 80–81); a 9-stage voltage-controlled ring oscillator (8 current-starved inverters + enabling NAND to satisfy Barkhausen) (pp. 81–83).

**(a) OCI digital isolation — modulation schemes (pp. 83–89).** The isolation chain is modulator → on-chip transformer (OCI) → demodulator.
- *Amplitude modulation (AM)* (p. 83–84): input enables the VCRO and supplies two complementary modulating inverters, driving the OCI with ±VDD bursts for the duration of the input high state.
- *Pulse modulation (PM)* (pp. 84–85, proposed in this work): an XOR of the input and a delayed copy emits one narrow pulse at each edge; only edges are transmitted, eliminating the standing oscillator loss of AM. Compared with the literature's set/reset (needs two OCIs), pulse-polarity (needs negative-level demodulation) and pulse-count (needs edge discrimination) schemes, the proposed PM needs one OCI, no negative levels, and no edge-type detection (p. 85).
- *Pulse-train modulation (PTM)* (pp. 85–86, proposed): the XOR pulse instead gates the oscillator, sending a burst of high-frequency pulses per edge — redundancy for robustness at modest extra loss.
- *Demodulators* (pp. 86–89): for AM, a diode rectifier-and-hold is replaced by a proposed PMOS-based peak-hold (three inverters + PMOS instead of four inverters + diode) that removes reverse-recovery droop and shrinks area (Fig. 3.24, p. 87). For PM/PTM, a proposed toggling demodulator: OCI pulses clock a D flip-flop whose D input is a delayed, inverted copy of its own output; a weak pull-down defines the start-up state (Fig. 3.25, p. 88).

**(a) OCI transformer construction (pp. 89–95).** Maximum footprint 500 µm set by layout. Process offers six metal layers — thick metal 5, ultra-thick metal 6; top-metal design rules: 5 µm minimum width, 3 µm spacing. Design flow (Fig. 3.26, p. 90): HFSS electromagnetic model → Touchstone .s2p → Virtuoso/Spectre co-simulation → iterate → GDS into the chip layout. Three winding arrangements were characterised in HFSS (Figs. 3.27–3.28, pp. 91–92): (aʹ) side-by-side on metal 6 (5 µm/12 turns; 7 µm/10 turns; 500 × 250 µm) — adequate Q and self-resonance but very low coupling (k ≈ 0.1–0.3); (bʹ) stacked metal 6 over metal 5 (7 µm/10 and 20 turns; 14 µm/5 and 10 turns) — high k but poor secondary Q (thin metal 5) and low self-resonance from interwinding capacitance; (cʹ) **interleaved, both windings on top metal** (7 µm/5 and 10 turns; 14 µm/3 and 6 turns; 500 × 500 µm) — best compromise: top-metal Q, k ≈ 0.9–0.98, sidewall-only parasitic capacitance. **Selected: interleaved, 7 µm trace, ten turns, 500 µm** — stable to the intended operating range "up to 100 MHz and, if feasible, up to 0.5 GHz" (p. 92–94). Dummy-metal fill beneath the transformer was separately simulated: slight inductance/Q degradation only at high frequency, negligible in-band (Figs. 3.29–3.30, pp. 94–95). A shrink study (Fig. 3.35(b), p. 100) showed a 300 µm/7 µm/7-turn device still works while a 100 µm/7 µm/2-turn device induces too little secondary voltage to drive the demodulator — bounding the minimum viable OCI size between 100 and 300 µm in this process.

**Simulation results (post-parasitic extraction, Spectre; pp. 95–112).**
- VCRO: control voltage 1–4.2 V sweeps oscillation from **≈500 kHz to 500 MHz** (Fig. 3.31, p. 96), power consumption rising to hundreds of mW at the top of the range.
- AM half-bridge: rising delay ≈2 ns; falling delay ≈790 ns (RC discharge dominated) (Fig. 3.36, p. 101). Dead time correctly applied (Fig. 3.37, p. 102); integrated power-switch R_ON ≈ 75–105 mΩ over 2.6–4.4 V (p. 102) — high only because die area was shared among five experiments; the text notes a production part would simply upsize the switches (p. 102).
- PM half-bridge: rise/fall delays 1.73 ns / 1.84 ns (Fig. 3.40, p. 104).
- PTM: 550 ns edge pulses gate the oscillator; demodulator triggers on the first burst pulse, the rest are redundancy (p. 105).
- **Comparative isolation study (Table 3.2, p. 107)** for a 10 µs/5 µs input, V_ctrl = 1.2 V: rising delay 2 / 1.73 / 1.73 ns and falling delay 790 / 1.84 / 1.84 ns for AM/PM/PTM; total isolation loss **335.04 mW (AM), 42.15 mW (PM), 117.9 mW (PTM)** — PM cuts total loss 87.4 % and modulator loss 92.4 % versus AM; PTM cuts total loss 64.8 %.
- Four-level MMC: staircase synthesis across three stacked cells verified; level-shifter rise delays 1.225/1.018/0.728 ns and fall delays 3.48/4.02/4.538 ns for levels 1–3 (Fig. 3.45, p. 111).

**Fabrication and packaging (pp. 112–115).** Die: **4 mm × 4 mm** in 130 nm BCD carrying all five circuits (chip micrograph Fig. 3.47, p. 112: 1 four-level MMC, 2 half-bridge PTM, 3 five-level CHB, 4 half-bridge PM, 5 half-bridge AM). Interconnect: 50 µm aluminium wedge-bonded wire to a cartridge PCB. Supply-bounce analysis (Fig. 3.50–3.51, pp. 113–114): parasitic-inductance spikes exceed device ratings; suppressed with a 22 µF off-chip plus a 50 pF on-chip decoupling capacitor. Two wire-bonding plans evaluate (i) MMC + AM half-bridge and (ii) CHB + PM + PTM half-bridges (Fig. 3.52, p. 115). Test rig: cartridge board (die, decoupling, gold fingers) into a motherboard with fibre-optic control-signal delivery and terminal-block power (pp. 115–116).

**(b) Experimental validation (pp. 115–125).**
- *Five-level CHB* (pp. 115–117): two 3 V cells, externally supplied gate signals with 50 ns dead time, full five-level ±6 V output at 50 Hz (Fig. 3.55); measured leg transition delays **7.6 ns rise / 4 ns fall** (Fig. 3.56, p. 117). The floating-substrate CHB configuration therefore works in silicon.
- *Four-level MMC* (pp. 118–119): one command per level, internal level-shifting/dead-time/gate-drive; output steps 0/3/6/9 V at 50 Hz (Fig. 3.57); level order interchangeable.
- *Half-bridge, AM* (pp. 119–123): functional at 10 kHz (4 V supply/cell). Measured delays: **38.4 ns rising, 498.8 ns falling** (Fig. 3.59, p. 120). Maximum-rate demonstration: a **1.5 MHz** input at duty 0.1 is reproduced, though the asymmetric delays stretch output duty to ≈0.8 (Fig. 3.60, p. 120). Loaded tests from 100 Hz to 1.5 MHz into an R–L load (≈1.2 µH, 2.45–2.9 Ω, Fig. 3.61): at 100 Hz the board delivers 1.44 A with 0.33 V drop (p. 120–121); body-diode conduction during dead time observed at −0.15 V and analysed up to 500 kHz (Fig. 3.64, p. 122).
- *Half-bridge, PM* (pp. 123–124): delays **39 ns rising / 36.4 ns falling** — the falling delay improves from 498.8 ns to 36.4 ns versus AM. Stable operation demonstrated up to **418 kHz**; the naïve limit from the 3 µs demodulator feedback delay would be 166 kHz, and the measured 418 kHz implies an effective feedback delay of ≈1.2 µs, attributed to MOM-capacitor behaviour (pp. 123–124, Fig. 3.67).
- *Half-bridge, PTM* (pp. 124–125): rise and fall delays both **≈40 ns**, matching PM as expected.

**Conclusion (pp. 125–126).** Integration levels, five circuit topologies, all constituent blocks, and simulation-plus-silicon validation are delivered; Ch. 7 adds: "The pulse modulation method effectively eliminates the need for a high-frequency oscillator and a high-falling-delay demodulator, enabling low-loss and high-frequency digital isolation" (p. 217).

### Chapter 4 — Equalized Energy Extraction in MLIs: Minimizing Imbalance (pp. 127–166)

**Claim.** Preventive, sensor-free modulation can equalise energy extraction across cells in multilevel inverters, avoiding SoC imbalance before it arises; two methods are proposed, needing no voltage/current measurement and no hardware change.

**Method 1 — Decomposed Sweeping Window (DSW) modulation (pp. 130–148)**, for isolated-cell MLIs (CHB/MMC). The modulating vector of a circular nearest-level-control representation is decomposed into two vectors bounding a "window" of active cells that sweeps around the ring; the sweep-speed law (Eqs. 4.16–4.22) holds the ratio of instantaneous power to rotation speed constant so all cells yield equal energy. A coefficient k sets rotations per period; choosing k with 1/k irreducible and a large denominator (k = 0.3, 1.3 → direction period 10T; k = 0.33 → 100T) maximises condition diversity — k = 1.3 gives a cell 18 distinct contribution sections versus 3 for k = 3 (pp. 139–141). Simulation (20-cell single-phase CHB, 20 V cells, 75 kVA at 0.8 lag, pp. 141–143): charge-variance bounded and periodic (period 10 cycles) versus monotonic growth under NLC; switching losses rise with k; self-heating (max squared RMS cell current) falls ~5–80 % depending on output voltage (p. 143). **Experiments** (pp. 143–148): 12 Keithley 2281S-20-6 battery simulators feeding a 25-level CHB into 16 Ω at 0.1 Hz (simulator SoC-reporting limit), LV/MV/HV ≈10/30/60 V, run from 100 % until any cell hits 65 % SoC. LV durations (20 mAh/cell): NLC 75 s; swapping 487 s; DSW k = 1.3 476 s (Fig. 4.18, p. 145). Mean coefficient of variation of SoC profiles (Fig. 4.19, p. 146): LV 0.0604 (NLC) / 0.0252 (swapping) / 0.0076, 0.0051, 0.0021 (DSW k = 1.3, 3.3, 9.3); MV 0.0861 / 0.0112 / 0.0081, 0.0037, 0.0014; HV 0.0514 / 0.0079 / 0.0078, 0.0024, 0.0025. Cost: THD rises from 7.45 % (NLC/swapping) to 10.54 % (k = 1.3) and 10.25 % (k = 3.3) (p. 146). Chapter 7 summarises this as "up to a 98 % improvement in state-of-charge (SoC) balancing compared to standard methods" on the 25-level prototype (p. 218).

**Method 2 — Adaptive Harmonic Injection (AHI) modulation (pp. 148–166)**, for series-connected-cell MLIs (no bypass capability). Third-harmonic injection is generalised: instead of the fixed k = 1/6 that merely maximises output voltage, the coefficient is recomputed for each commanded amplitude (cubic Eq. 4.29) so the resultant phase control voltage always spans the full ±V_DC/2, engaging *every* cell at *every* output level; the common-mode term cancels line-to-line. Sinusoidal and triangular injection waveforms are both derived (Eqs. 4.30–4.36), each superior at different operating points. Standalone simulation (12 levels, 4 V cells, 8–44 V, 1 Ω): CV of extracted energy cut ≈85 %, 80 % and 65 % at 2, 4 and 6 V_cell outputs (p. 157). Combined with the generalised MLI's intrinsic capacitor self-balancing (Table 4.3, p. 158: 200 µF/2 mF capacitors, 2 kHz/200 kHz transition frequencies), the proposed method at low capacitance and low frequency outperforms the conventional method even at high capacitance and high frequency for LV and MV (p. 159); load-supply switching losses rise ≈180 %/100 %/21 % (LV/MV/HV) while balancing conduction/switching losses fall 98 %/89 % (LV) and 71 %/61.5 % (MV) (p. 160). **Experiments** (pp. 162–165; Table 4.4, p. 163): 12-cell system on Keithley 2281S-20-6 simulators (Samsung 18650 3.7 V model, 6 Ah), B-Box RCP 3.0 controller, TPH1R405PL MOSFETs, outputs 8/16/32 V_RMS into 2/8/32 Ω, 3-minute runs; adaptive k = 0.2974/1.276/2.7584 (sinusoidal) and 3.1742/1.3371/0.4186 (triangular). At 8 V, NLC draws from only four of twelve cells (B5–B8, up to I_DC = 3.164 A) while AHI spreads near-identical currents across all twelve (I_DC ≈ 0.49–1.16 A) (Fig. 4.37, p. 163). Used-capacity variance (Table 4.5, p. 164): 8 V — 4×10⁻³ mAh² (NLC) vs 1.14×10⁻⁴ (sin) and 6.79×10⁻⁵ (tri); 16 V — 1.3×10⁻³ vs 1.38×10⁻⁴ and 3.63×10⁻⁴; 32 V — all converge (5.99–8.28×10⁻⁵). THD remains comparable across methods (p. 166). Chapter 7 records validation "on a 12-cell 48 V system under multiple operating conditions (8 V, 16 V, and 32 V)" (p. 218).

### Chapter 5 — Proper Active Balancing Solution: Fully Balance Realization (pp. 167–190)

**Claim (d).** The proposed Complementary Star Switched-Capacitor Active Balancer (CS2CAB) achieves fast, sensor-less, location-independent, continuous-current balancing compatible with both series-connected and isolated cells — properties no prior switched-capacitor balancer combines.

**Topology and control (pp. 169–170).** Each cell gets an H-bridge; each H-bridge output leg feeds one plate of a capacitor whose other plate joins a shared star node (two stars: upper and lower legs). Control is only **two complementary 0.5-duty pulses** for the whole pack. Static analysis (pp. 170–173) yields the governing result (Eq. 5.13): I_Bk = (C/Δt)(V_k − V̄), i.e. every cell's current is set solely by its voltage relative to the pack average — cells above average discharge, below average charge, at average carry zero current — "independent of its location and the state of adjacent cells" (p. 173). Dynamic analysis (pp. 173–175) gives closed-form capacitor voltage/current including ESR, R_DS(on) and cell resistance (Eqs. 5.19–5.21).

**Design considerations (pp. 175–179).** DC balancing current grows with frequency and capacitance (example: two cells at 5 V/4 V, r_e = 30 mΩ, up to ~8 A over 0–800 µF, 0–300 kHz; Fig. 5.5, p. 176). Conduction loss analysis uses the measured frequency-dependent ESR of a 100 µF MLCC (70 mΩ at 100 Hz → 3.1 mΩ at 36 kHz → 9 mΩ at 800 kHz; Fig. 5.6, p. 176). Maximum frequency is bounded by capacitor self-resonance: ≈300 kHz–1 MHz for the 22–100 µF MLCCs surveyed (Fig. 5.7, p. 177). Optimum operating point: the lowest frequency that yields continuous, low-ripple cell current (conduction-deficiency index dʹ = I²_RMS/I_DC flattens there; higher frequency only adds switching loss) (pp. 177–179).

**Comparative analysis (pp. 179–185).** Four-cell string [3.2, 3.4, 3.8, 4.0 V]: only CS2CAB gives continuous cell currents; DC/RMS 18.64/19.45 A versus S3CAB-system3's 9.38/13.78 A — roughly double the DC current at the same capacitance, with the best DC-to-RMS ratio (Figs. 5.11–5.12, pp. 179–180). Twenty-cell SPICE case studies (10 mΩ cells, 560 µF/50 mΩ capacitors, 33 µH/50 mΩ inductors where applicable, Si7234DP models, 50 kHz) across four initial-voltage patterns: CS2CAB alone is insensitive to cell ordering, with the highest mean currents and shortest balancing times, though not the lowest balancing energy (higher RMS current and component count) (Figs. 5.13–5.15, pp. 181–182). Table 5.1 (p. 183): CS2CAB uses 4n switches at cell voltage and 2n capacitors, no inductors, speed/loss H/M, and is the only compared balancer supporting isolated cells. Connection independence is shown on a 4-cell/9-level CHB whose cells re-group continuously during modulation (pp. 183–184). Robustness: ±20 % deviations in cell resistance and capacitance shift initial I_DC from 13.85 A to 12.26 A and efficiency from 91.83 % to 91.38 % worst case (Table 5.2, p. 185).

**(d) Experimental results (pp. 185–189).** Five-cell rig on Keithley 2281S-20-6 simulators (Samsung 18650 model scaled to 20 mAh), FDS8978 MOSFETs, 2 × 100 µF C1210C107M4PACTU per leg, at 1/5/10 kHz; initial SoCs {86, 74, 70, 80, 90 %} and a reordered variant. CS2CAB reaches the 2 % maximum-ΔSoC criterion in **37 s at 10 kHz, 90 s at 5 kHz, 580 s at 1 kHz**, identically for both cell orders and with monotonic SoC trajectories (no unwanted charge/discharge intervals) (Figs. 5.20–5.22, pp. 186–187). The S3CAB-system3 benchmark at 10 kHz needs **330 s (favourable order) or 750 s (unfavourable order)** — CS2CAB is ~9–20× faster and order-independent (pp. 187–188). Characterisation on a two-cell unit (Table 5.4, p. 188: Keithley source 5 V, Keithley 2380 sink at 4.7/4.9 V, Si7234DP, 300/900 µF, 1–100 kHz): DC output current follows the model, and efficiency rises with frequency, plateaus at ≈90 % between 5 and 25 kHz where current becomes continuous, then falls from switching loss (Fig. 5.24, p. 189).

### Chapter 6 — Low Leakage Transformers: Perfectly Coupled Magnetics (pp. 191–215)

**Claim.** For magnetic active balancers needing low-impedance n:n transformers, a fully-interleaved *parallel–parallel* foil-winding arrangement (interleaving both between windings and among paralleled sheets within each turn) minimises leakage inductance and AC resistance; an explicit Maxwell-equation analytical model — valid for any geometry and frequency, with no thin-conductor or frequency assumptions — predicts both.

**Model (pp. 194–203).** 1-D Helmholtz solution per foil sheet; 2n field coefficients solved from four boundary-condition families (zero H at the core-side surface, H continuity across gaps, Faraday loops between paralleled sheets, and an Ampère loop or total-current integral), assembled as structured 2n × 2n matrices (Eqs. 6.17, 6.18, 6.28) for the non-interleaved and fully-interleaved cases. Leakage inductance follows from stored energy with the secondary shorted; AC resistance from the Poynting vector; n:n values are the 1:1 values times n (pp. 201–202).

**Findings (pp. 203–210).** FEA/analytical field maps agree at 500 Hz/200 kHz/1 MHz (Figs. 6.3–6.4). Sample study (0.5 mm total conductor, 160 mm height): four paralleled sheets carry at 1 MHz the current a single sheet carries at 50 kHz (p. 206); at 10 MHz four-sheet AC resistance is 7× lower and impedance up to 85 % lower than single-sheet (pp. 207–209). A skin-depth rule (Eq. 6.39) recommends 1/2/3/4 sheets up to 18/70/160/280 kHz for this case (p. 208). An equi-impedance design chart trades copper usage against maximum frequency (Fig. 6.9, p. 209): 0.3 mΩ is met by one 0.2 mm sheet at 200 kHz or by four paralleled sheets of the same total copper at ≈4 MHz.

**Validation (pp. 210–215).** Two prototypes (Table 6.1, p. 211): non-interleaved (1 × 200 µm sheet per winding) and fully-interleaved (4 × 50 µm), both 4.8 mm wide, 2 m long, 70 µm polyimide insulation, FLP240/60/8-BH1T ferrite slabs, 40 mm × 10 mm window; measured with a Keysight E4990A in five-terminal configuration over 20 Hz–1 MHz (values below 50 kHz excluded for analyser accuracy). Results: full interleaving cuts leakage inductance by **≈90 % across the whole band** and makes it nearly frequency-flat; reactance reduction ≈120 mΩ at 200 kHz and ≈400 mΩ at 1 MHz; resistance reduction <5 % below 100 kHz rising to **≈60 % at 800 kHz** (p. 214). Model accuracy: resistance error <10 % against measurement throughout; leakage error <15 % (non-interleaved), exceeding 20 % only below 150 kHz for the interleaved part (analyser-limited around 10 nH); against FEA, errors <1 % (interleaved, except <4 % at low frequency) and <5 % (non-interleaved) (Fig. 6.15, pp. 213–214).

## 5. Limitations / future work as stated

- The discrete UPU is a single-unit demonstrator; a three-phase multi-unit traction/charging system, cooling characterisation, and volumetric/gravimetric benchmarking remain to be done (pp. 216–217).
- The ICs are validated as circuits, not yet co-packaged with cells into a working UPU; power devices are deliberately undersized (shared 4 mm × 4 mm research die; R_ON ≈ 65–110 mΩ) and higher-power devices, broader functional integration, and heterogeneous power/control technologies are all future work (pp. 102, 217–218).
- CHB integration relies on a floating substrate whose behaviour was de-risked with a bond-out SUB pin and then shown working, but was flagged pre-silicon as requiring experimental verification (p. 73).
- AM isolation has an intrinsically asymmetric response (498.8 ns falling delay, duty distortion at 1.5 MHz); PM is frequency-limited to 418 kHz in this implementation by the demodulator feedback delay, with the measured/theoretical discrepancy (1.2 µs vs 3 µs) attributed to MOM-capacitor variability (pp. 120, 123–124).
- DSW improves balance at the cost of higher switching loss and THD (7.45 % → ~10.5 %); AHI raises load-supply switching losses (up to +180 % at low voltage) and its balancing-loss advantage inverts at high output voltage (pp. 143, 146, 160).
- Ch. 4 experiments ran at 0.1 Hz fundamental because the battery simulators cannot report SoC in real time at speed (stated as having "no bearing on the results", p. 144).
- CS2CAB uses the most switches of the compared balancers (4n) and its balancing energy loss is not the lowest despite the shortest balancing time (pp. 182–183).
- The Ch. 6 model assumes field homogeneity along the sheet height (edge effects neglected); accuracy degrades as width-to-thickness ratio falls, and sub-50 kHz measurements were analyser-limited (pp. 213–214).

## 6. Proposal mapping

**WP2 — control-signal isolation across stacked floating domains (RQ2).** This thesis is the group's silicon-proven evidence that gate-command isolation can be built *inside* a submodule IC with a coreless on-chip transformer, no external isolator, and no per-domain isolated supply: a 500 µm, ten-turn, 7 µm-trace interleaved top-metal transformer in 130 nm BCD, three modulation schemes designed and fabricated, and measured edge delays of ≈38–40 ns (AM rise 38.4 ns; PM 39/36.4 ns; PTM ≈40 ns both edges), with AM demonstrated passing a 1.5 MHz input and PM stable to 418 kHz; PM cuts isolation power 87.4 % versus AM (Table 3.2, p. 107). For the per-slot drive, where each slot converter sits in its own series-stacked 20–50 V domain, exactly this class of floating-domain signal path is what RQ2 must scale: the thesis supplies the validated modulation/demodulation circuit family, the transformer design flow (HFSS→Spectre→GDS) and sizing bounds (viable between ~300 µm and 500 µm; 100 µm fails), the dummy-metal and decoupling (22 µF off-chip + 50 pF on-chip) integration lessons, and the known limitation (PM feedback-delay frequency ceiling) that WP2 improves upon. It also pairs naturally with the group's TCAS-I multiple-output level shifter work: the thesis's own MMC uses level shifters with sub-5 ns delays for stacked references sharing a ground, and the OCI where no common reference exists — together spanning the two isolation regimes a per-slot stack needs.
**Integration-boundary principle for WP-level partitioning.** The Ch. 3 rule — integrate until repeated control/support circuits are eliminated, but keep lateral power currents out of IC metal (pp. 69–71) — transfers directly to deciding what belongs on a per-slot die (gate drive, dead time, isolation, monitoring) versus the slot busbar (phase current), and is quotable as prior in-house design methodology.
**The UPU-to-per-slot conceptual bridge (Vision).** The thesis's central move is granular co-location: pair each energy element (cell) with its own processing electronics, make units identical and stackable, absorb the functions of centralised converter blocks, and win on scalability, fault bypass, waveform quality, partial-load efficiency and serviceability (pp. 34–36). The per-slot proposal performs the mirrored move on the machine side: pair each *stator slot* (electromagnetic element) with its own integrated converter section, with the same claimed dividends — low-voltage devices in a series stack, graceful degradation, and removal of the centralised inverter. The Impact Statement's phrasing that the work "aims to advance the consideration of cell-scale power processing concepts across a range of applications" (p. 3) and the further-work call to extend the concept to other device/control technologies (p. 218) provide the thesis's own warrant for that extension. Supporting numbers usable in the Vision's evidence base (anonymised as "the group's work"): silicon-validated 5-level CHB / 4-level MMC / isolated half-bridges in 130 nm BCD; 98 % SoC-balancing improvement via measurement-free modulation on a 25-level prototype (p. 218); CS2CAB balancing in 37 s versus 330–750 s for the prior art (pp. 186–188); and 90 % leakage-inductance reduction with a validated analytical magnetics model (p. 214) — collectively demonstrating the group's ability to carry distributed power processing from concept through IC design to experimental proof.
